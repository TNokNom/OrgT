* Audio Signal
- An audio signal is transmitted either as a voltage or as a current (*Voltage is more convenient*)
** Modes 
- There are two common modes for audio outputs that are used
*** Voltage Mode
- Low output impedance and high input impedance
*** Current Mode
- High output impedance and low input impedance 
- Meaning it can drive fewer loads and the receiving device responds primarily to the current
**** Problem with current mode
- If a device with a current output is not connected to anything, the voltage can exceed normal levels, leading to clipping where the signal distorts.
- Voltage outputs don't typically face this issue

* Negative Feedback
- Used in control gain, to reduce distortion and improve frequency response,
  and to set input and output impedance
- Comes in four basic modes that can be taken from the output in two different ways
  - *Voltage or current feedback*
- And applied to the input in two different ways
  - *Series or Shunt*
** Main Rules
- The feedback is almost always taken as voltage from the output
- You will typically only using series and shunt feedback systems
 
*** Rules for series feedback in high gain voltage amplifiers
-> Negative feedback reduces voltage gain
-> Negative feedback increases gain stability
-> Negative feedback increases bandwidth
-> Negative feedback increase amplifier input impedance
-> Negative feedback reduces amplifier output impedance
-> Negative feedback reduces distortion
-> Negative feedback does not directly alter the signal-to-noise ratio
**** Equations
=> \( \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{A}{1 + A \beta} \)

* Nominal Signal Levels
- When looking at the noise in a circuit its important not to get caught up in it
  what matters is the how much greater the signal is than the noise coming in
- So essentially the *signal-to-noise ratio*
- Its important to choose a signal level that doesn't fall to these two evils
  -> If the signal level is too low, it will be contaminated by noise.
  -> if the signal level is too high, there is a risk it will clip and introduce severe distortion.

* Noise
-  Noise refers to the random noise generated by resistances and active devices
- typically when noise is referred to in electronics we talk about *White Noise*
** Noises Types:
- non-white noises run on a color spectrum
   red noises have larger low-frequency content that white noises and pink is midway
    between the two
*** White Noises
- Has equal power in equal absolute bandwidth
- So the same power between 100 and 200 Hz as there is between 1100 and 1200 Hz (*Difference*)
- It is the type produced by most electronic noise mechanisms
*** Pink Noise
- Has equal power in equal ratios of bandwidth
- So the same power between 100 and 200 Hz as there is between 200 and 400 Hz (*Ratios*)
*** Red Noise/Brownian Noise 
- Has energy per Hz falling at 6 dB per octave rather than 3
- Has little application in audio
*** Blue Noise
- Has energy per Hz rising at 3 dB per octave
- Also no use in audio
** Johnson And Snot
- Both are forms of white noise
- Both have a Gaussian probability density functions
- The noise happens in both BJT's and FET's but in different ways

*** Johnson Noise
- Also refereed to as thermal noise
- Is produced by all resistances /Including those real resistances that come with transistors/
- It is present at some level in every circuit 
- Puts a theoretical limit on noise performance
- The rms amplitude of Johnson Noise is => \(V_{n} = \sqrt{4kTRB}\)
- \(V_{n}\) is -> The rms noise voltage
- \(T\) is -> the absolute temperature \(K\)
- \(B\) is -> the bandwidth in Hz
- \(k\) is -> Boltzmann's constant
-  \(R\) is -> the resistance
- Johnson Noise is typically represented as a voltage but it can also be presented by
  Johnson Noise Current => \(i_{n} = \frac{v_{n}}{R}\)
*** Snot Noise
-> Noise current \(i_{n} = \sqrt{ZqI_{dc}B} \)
****  Flicker Noise {1/f}
- Is a noise characterized by its power spectral density that is inversely proportional
  to the frequency of the signal
- Flicker noise has greater intensity at lower frequencies and decreases as frequency increases
- The exact mechanisms behind flicker noise are not known 
**** Popcorn Noise
- A type of low-frequency noise that appears primarily at low level step changes in the
  output voltage, occurring at random intervals
- Can often be temperature dependent (*Higher temperatures can increase the noise*)
**** Summing Noise Sources
- When summing noises you cant add them the same way you do \(3 + 3 = 6\)
- Since the noise components come from different sources some will combine and some will cancel out
  so we use the root-mean-square
-> \( V_{ntot} = \sqrt{V^{2}_{n1}+{V^{2}_{n2} +}{...}}   \)
** Perfect Noiseless Amplifier
- This is an idealized concept
- *Infinite gain*, meaning it could amplify a signal of any amplitude without distortion
  or saturation
- *No added noise*,meaning there is no introduction of any noise of its own
- *Infinite bandwidth*,meaning it could amplify signals of all frequencies
