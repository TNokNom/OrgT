** Important Notes 
+ Analog Best Practice: For precision analog design (like current mirrors, differential pairs, or op-amps), you should almost never use the absolute minimum length of 0.15 μm anyway. Short-channel effects and threshold voltage (Vth​) mismatch are quite severe at 150 nm in this process. Designers typically scale the length up to at least 0.5 μm or 1 μm for better output impedance (ro​) and matching.

* Layout considerations  
Choosing the right number of fingers is one of those classic "analog design trade-offs" where you balance layout geometry, speed, and noise.

There isn't a single mathematical formula that spits out the perfect number of fingers, but there are a few strict practical layout rules and electrical guidelines you can follow to decide.
1. The Layout Rule of Thumb: Aspect Ratio

A good layout should look like a neat square or a reasonable rectangle. If you have a massive transistor with a total width (Wtotal​) of 40 μm and a length of 0.15 μm, drawing it as a single finger (1×40/0.15) results in a long, skinny "wire" of a transistor that is a nightmare to route.

    The Fix: Break it into fingers. If you use 10 fingers, each finger is 4 μm wide (10×4/0.15). This clusters the gates closely together, creating a square-shaped layout that is much easier to wire up and fits neatly next to other components.

    Target Finger Width: In the SkyWater 130nm process, keeping individual finger widths (Wfinger​) between 1 μm and 5 μm is usually a safe, sweet spot for analog routing.

2. The Electrical Reason: Reducing Parasitics (Rg​ and Cdb​)

Splitting a transistor into fingers fundamentally changes its physics for the better in two ways:
A. Lowering Gate Resistance (Rg​)

Polysilicon (the gate material) acts like a resistor. If a single finger is too wide, the electrical signal takes time to travel from the contact point down to the end of the gate.  

    By using multiple shorter fingers and tying them together at both ends, you drastically reduce the gate resistance (Rg​). This makes the transistor switch much faster.

B. Sharing Drains to Reduce Capacitance (Cdb​)

When you lay out multiple fingers, they sit side-by-side and can actually share diffusion regions (the source and drain pools).

Instead of every finger needing its own dedicated source and drain, two adjacent fingers can share a single drain pool.

    Why this matters: Less drain area means less parasitic junction capacitance (Cdb​). Lower capacitance directly translates to a faster circuit and higher bandwidth.

3. Matching and Symmetry (For Differential Pairs & Mirrors)

If you are designing a circuit where two transistors must be identical (like a differential pair or a current mirror), you should always use an even number of fingers (e.g., 2, 4, 8).

    Why? An even number of fingers allows you to create a perfectly symmetrical layout layout technique (like "Common Centroid").

    This ensures that manufacturing variations across the silicon chip affect both transistors exactly the same way, preventing offsets.

How to approach it step-by-step:

    Calculate your total width (Wtotal​) required to get your target current or gain from your SPICE simulations.

    Divide that width by a reasonable finger size (e.g., 2 μm or 4 μm).

    Round to a clean integer (and pick an even number if it's for a matching pair).

For example, if your simulation says you need a total width of 24 μm:

    Instead of 1×24/0.15

    Go with 6×4/0.15 (6 fingers, each 4 μm wide) or 8×3/0.15.
